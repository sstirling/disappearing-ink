People don’t know how to react to incurable illness.

Most people are well meaning. Their instinct is to offer to help.

But generally, they do so before thinking that through.

Ultimately, most people can’t offer much.

I struggled with anger for a while when Carol’s symptoms became apparent but the news of her diagnosis hadn’t yet spread.

I’d have the same conversation again and again. How sad it was. How much they loved Carol. There was always an offer to help.

When it became clear Carol might have cancer, I heard the same thing at work. Reality set in that I might need some time off. Cancer treatment is grueling and often not geographically convenient.

Every single person offered what I believe was sincere sympathy. Most said some version of “we’re here to help.” But what I quickly learned is the gulf between those statements and what my company could actually offer was wide.

Where I work, I’m only entitled to FMLA leave – 12 weeks of unpaid leave with a guarantee my job will be there at the other end. I have 1,000 hours of sick time at 65% pay, but under the grounds of our guild contract I’m ineligible to use it.

It’s no secret that cancer treatment is expensive. While this was all being explained to me, again by people who were very sorry, I felt like screaming: How does this help?

I’m in a good situation. Carol should have the money to pay for treatment. We’re in a privileged position. I’m grateful. But I cannot be the only one in this situation and, in all likelihood, most don’t have the same assurances.
