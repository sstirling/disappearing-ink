The initial feeling wasn’t sadness or grief. It wasn’t even anger. Those things come later. It’s worse.

The thing that consumed me when Carol told me of her Alzheimer’s diagnosis was a profound sense of helplessness.

I’m an American man. We seem to share an innate sense for wanting to fix things when we encounter trouble. To do something, rather than feeling the impact of an obstacle and processing it first. It can be helpful. It can also be terribly destructive – gestures vaguely at world.

Nonetheless, that’s where I went. I have to help Carol. I have to fix this.

I know enough about Alzheimer’s. I can help, but only so much. There’s no fixing this. No changing the trajectory. I’m walking downhill with Carol for the rest of her life. It will only get worse. And she will die, likely a shell of who I knew her as.

For the first week, this was paralyzing.

But I need you to know it is temporary. It seems so antithetical, but once you accept what you’re facing, so does everything else.

Carol is still there. She’s a softer version of the person I’ve known, more vulnerable. She needs me. We both know this without saying it. I’ve made the choice to walk through this with her.

As a result, moments with her become far deeper, more meaningful. We’re closer. Most days there’s a lightness to our conversation that wasn’t there before, eclipsing when she was increasingly paranoid because she didn’t understand what was happening to her.

The path we’re walking may be downhill. But any moment can lift us both well above that.
